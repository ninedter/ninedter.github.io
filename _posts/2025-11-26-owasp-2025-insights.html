The OWASP Top 10 is a cornerstone of application security, a critical reference that developers, security professionals, and organizations worldwide use to prioritize their efforts. With the release of the 2025 update, the immediate focus is often on what’s new, what’s moved, and what’s gone. But the real story isn’t just the final ranking — it’s the surprising trends and data-driven shifts happening behind the scenes.

This year, the data reveals a fascinating narrative about the evolution of web application risk. It shows us how some classic vulnerabilities are receding, how expert intuition is challenging raw data, and how the very definition of risk is becoming more sophisticated. Let’s look beyond the checklist to uncover five counter-intuitive takeaways from the 2025 OWASP Top 10.

Takeaway 1: The Old Guard is Fading: Injection Continues Its Slow Descent

For over a decade, ‘Injection’ was the archetypal web vulnerability, often holding the #1 spot and serving as the poster child for application insecurity. Its slow but steady decline is one of the most significant long-term trends in the industry.

In the 2025 list, Injection has dropped from its A03 position in 2021 to A05, even though the raw data alone would have placed it at #4. This continued descent is a powerful indicator of progress. It suggests that years of developer education, combined with the widespread adoption of modern frameworks that provide built-in defenses against classic injection flaws, are having a measurable, positive impact on the security landscape.

Takeaway 2: The Data and the Community See Different Dangers

The final OWASP Top 10 list is a carefully crafted blend of two distinct perspectives: hard data gathered from millions of application scans and a community survey reflecting the forward-looking concerns of security professionals. This year, the gap between those two viewpoints is wider than ever, revealing a crucial tension between what is currently found and what is most feared.

The most striking discrepancy is for ‘Software Supply Chain Failures’. While the raw data ranked it at 10th, the community survey voted it as the single most critical risk, ranking it #1. A similar story unfolded for ‘Logging & Alerting Failures’, which ranked 11th in the data (just outside the Top 10) but was voted as the #3 most critical risk by the community. The same pattern held for ‘Software or Data Integrity Failures,’ which the data placed at a modest 9th place, but the community survey voted as the #2 most critical risk, ultimately keeping it on the list at A08. This dual-source approach demonstrates that the list is more than a snapshot of today’s vulnerabilities; it’s a strategic tool that balances the reality of automated findings with the expert foresight of practitioners on the front lines.

Takeaway 3: An Epidemiological Approach: It’s Not How Often, It’s How Widespread

One of the most sophisticated aspects of the Top 10’s methodology is its shift away from simple frequency counting. Instead of just tallying the total number of times a vulnerability appears, the analysis uses an approach inspired by epidemiology to focus on a more meaningful metric: Incidence Rate.

Incidence Rate measures the percentage of the total application population that has at least one instance of a specific vulnerability. This is a more accurate way to measure risk because it highlights how widespread a problem is across the entire software ecosystem. For example, OWASP prefers to measure risk this way: a vulnerability found once in 1,000 different applications is a more systemic risk than a vulnerability found 1,000 times in just 10 applications. This epidemiological approach gives a truer picture of which risks are most pervasive.

Takeaway 4: The More Things Change, The More They Stay the Same: Broken Access Control Remains #1

Amidst all the shifts and new entries, one thing has remained constant: ‘Broken Access Control’ has retained its #1 position from the 2021 list. This category represents failures in enforcing policies that control what users are allowed to do. In simple terms, it’s about making sure users can’t access functions or data that they shouldn’t.

The fact that this remains the most critical application security risk is a sobering reminder for the industry. Despite all the focus on complex new attack vectors and emerging technologies, we still struggle with getting this fundamental security principle right. It underscores that mastering the basics is, and continues to be, the most important aspect of application security.

Takeaway 5: The New Landscape: Misconfigurations and Supply Chain Risks Surge

While some risks fall, others are rapidly rising to take their place, painting a clear picture of the modern development landscape. Beyond the big climbers, other key categories shifted, with ‘Cryptographic Failures’ falling from #2 to #4 and ‘Insecure Design’ dropping from #4 to #6. These movements make way for the risks that define today’s primary battleground: configuration, infrastructure, and third-party dependencies.

  • Security Misconfiguration: This category made a significant jump from #5 in 2021 to #2 in 2025, showing that as systems become more complex, the potential for error in configuring them grows.
  • Software Supply Chain Failures: This category, an evolution of 2021’s ‘Vulnerable and Outdated Components,’ rose from #6 to #3. This elevation reflects the industry’s growing and acute awareness of the dangers lurking in third-party libraries and dependencies.
  • A New Face: A brand-new category, ‘Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions’, has appeared at #10. It enters the list by displacing 2021’s ‘Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF)’ and points to the growing need for more resilient code that can gracefully handle unexpected errors and states.

These shifts are a direct reflection of modern software development. As teams rely more on Infrastructure-as-Code, complex container orchestration, and a vast ecosystem of open-source dependencies, the potential for a single misconfigured setting or a vulnerable third-party library to have a catastrophic impact has surged.

Conclusion: A Compass, Not Just a Map

The 2025 OWASP Top 10 is not a static list; it’s the product of a dynamic tension between empirical data on current vulnerabilities and expert foresight into emerging threats. By understanding the methodology, the debates between data and community, and the trajectory of specific risks, we can use it not just as a map of current dangers, but as a compass to guide our future security strategies.
